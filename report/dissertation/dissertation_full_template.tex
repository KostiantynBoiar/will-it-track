\title{Predicting the Zero-Shot Transfer of a Promptable Video Tracker}
\author{Kostiantyn Boiar}
\date{\today}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Conservation teams increasingly monitor wildlife with camera traps, which record more footage than can be
reviewed manually. Locating each animal in a video and following it from frame to frame is the bottleneck,
and a new generation of promptable trackers is intended to remove it. Given a natural-language name for an
animal, the model finds and follows every instance that appears, without ever having been trained on that
species. SAM\,3 is the current example. The difficulty is that this ability varies in ways that cannot
currently be anticipated: high on one animal or location, low on the next. A team pointing such a model at a
new species therefore has no way of judging beforehand whether to trust its output or fall back on manual
annotation.

This dissertation asks whether that judgement can be made in advance. It tests a natural intuition: that a
model should perform worst on the animals and places least like those it has seen before. Were that true,
one could measure how unfamiliar a species or a setting is, at low cost, without annotating the target data
and without running the model at all, and use the measurement to forecast reliability. Four such measures of
unfamiliarity are tested, and the question is put separately to two distinct abilities: finding the animal,
and maintaining consistent identities once it has been found.

The answer is negative. On a large camera-trap benchmark of wild animals, no measure of unfamiliarity
predicts performance on species or places the model has not seen. The only property with predictive value is
how large the animal appears in frame, a nuisance covariate rather than a discovery. A second attempt based
on how difficult the footage looks, such as darkness and visual clutter, reduces to that same size effect.
The finding is not specific to one dataset or one model: it replicates on a second, independent dataset and
on two further trackers of quite different design. Nor is it a failure of method, since the same test
reliably detects the size effect whenever it is present.

The contribution is therefore a carefully bounded negative result, the first for this class of model. With
it comes a practical caution: resemblance to familiar training data is no guarantee that such a tracker will
perform adequately, and reliability has to be checked rather than assumed.
\end{abstract}
